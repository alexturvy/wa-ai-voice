# Audience Archetypes

Watershed deliverables generally land with one of five readers. The audience drives almost everything about how a piece works: what gets foregrounded, what register is appropriate, how much framing the work has to do, how editorial it can afford to be.

This is the substance behind failure #13 in `watershed-ece-voice.md`. Nasha's open question was whether audience awareness could be built into a skill. It can — this is how. Name the archetype before drafting, and check the draft against that archetype's anxieties, not just its interests.

**Internal reference.** The framing guidance below reflects how Watershed reads its clients. Use it to shape work; don't quote it into a deliverable.

---

## Governor's office / chief of staff

**Cares about.** Politics is the through-line. The political climate of their state, how a recommendation navigates it, how it ties to the platform they ran on. Whether and how this gets them a win.

**Anxious about.** Political exposure. Anything that conflicts with their stated agenda or reads as a partisan misread of their state. Recommendations they can't take a victory lap on.

**Framing that lands.** Plain language. Top-line: less detail, more direction. Talking points the principal could repeat in a stump speech. Heavy alignment to this governor's particular agenda. The same idea is framed very differently depending on the governor's party — a red-governor framing on child care leans economic-benefit-for-workforce; a blue-governor framing pulls different threads, and a red-state/blue-governor combination is different again. Recommendations positioned as wins the governor can claim.

**Framing that backfires.** Politically tone-deaf framing: language coded to the wrong party, or that sounds like generic policy advocacy rather than a tailored read of this state's politics. Detail-heavy text that doesn't land an executive-level point quickly. Recommendations that don't translate into something the governor can carry.

**Framing density.** Heavy. Much of what you're doing for this reader is framing, not substance.

**Editorial latitude.** Limited. Stay measured. This reader has political constraints the work has to respect.

## State agency director or commissioner

**Cares about.** Straddling. The political world is real for them, but they also have to do the work. Their team. How a recommendation plays out operationally and who it affects. They can see the weeds and they know what's an easy or tough sell upward.

**Anxious about.** Implementation risk. Team capacity. Whether leadership above them will back this. Whether they'll inherit a problem from a recommendation they signed off on.

**Framing that lands.** Asset-based language about what's already working in their system — they're the ones who have been doing this work. Recommendations framed as how they move the work forward, aligned to their priorities, that they can take a win on. Less critical of the agency than what works for funders or advocates.

**Framing that backfires.** Heavily critical framing of the system they're running or inheriting. Recommendations that read as outside critique without partnership. Anything that ignores the operational realities they manage.

**Framing density.** Moderate. They want substance and direction; framing matters but isn't doing as much of the work.

**Editorial latitude.** Limited to moderate. More measured than advocacy work; still incisive when warranted.

---

## Implementation lead and operational staff

**Cares about.** What agency leadership cares about, more granularly. The work itself: sequencing, dependencies, who does what, what the change looks like day to day.

**Anxious about.** Workload. Change disrupting things that already work. Recommendations they can't execute. Civil-service staff in particular, the layer that has been in those roles a long time, can be more change-resistant than the political appointees above them.

**Framing that lands.** Once an agency lead has signed off and you're packaging for the team, the framing softens: more change management, more building investment, more "you get to be part of this." Operational specificity. Clarity about what changes and what doesn't.

**Framing that backfires.** Top-down political framing. Recommendations that ignore operational reality. Anything reading as outsiders telling them how to do the work.

**Framing density.** Light. Content-heavy, framing-light — this reader is in the weeds with you.

**Editorial latitude.** Low. Constructive and grounded.

**Note on junior staff.** Sometimes the reader is a very junior staffer. Treat as implementation lead with a caveat: they may not be decision-makers, and the work shouldn't assume authority to act that they don't have.

---

## Advocacy coalition or partner organization

**Cares about.** What affects them and what they're advocating for. Narrower in focus than the policy-shaping readers: what does this mean for our constituency or issue.

**Anxious about.** Recommendations that don't go far enough, or that read as compromised by political proximity to power.

**Framing that lands.** The most editorial register of any reader. Be incisive, name systemic problems plainly. These are allies who often want sharper-edged framing than other readers tolerate. Connect what you're saying to what they're advocating for.

**Framing that backfires.** Excessive hedging. Toned-down language that reads as compromised. Generic systems-change framing that doesn't speak to their specific cause.

**Framing density.** Heavy. This reader cares about the ideological and political shape of a recommendation as much as the substance.

**Editorial latitude.** High. The most available across readers.

---

## Funder

**Cares about.** Outcomes. If they're putting money here, what will be true. The specific outcomes that matter for this funder's strategy and theory of change.

**Anxious about.** Spending where outcomes aren't clear. Recommendations disconnected from their investment thesis. Work that reads as generic policy rather than aligned to their priorities.

**Framing that lands.** Outcome-focused language. Connect the recommendation to what this funder cares about specifically — a national foundation focused on early literacy outcomes is different from a state-level family foundation. Slightly less editorial than advocacy work, slightly more measured, still substantive about what's needed.

**Framing that backfires.** Generic framing that doesn't reflect the funder's outcomes. Cost-heavy framing without outcome alignment. Treating the funder as an advocate when they're operating on a different theory of change.

**Framing density.** Heavy.

**Editorial latitude.** Moderate to high. Less than advocacy, more than government readers.

## Cross-cutting patterns

**The framing-density gradient.** How much framing a draft needs is not constant. Governor's office, advocacy, and funder need heavy framing — for those readers much of what the work does *is* framing. Agency leadership sits in the middle. Implementation leads need light framing, because they're already inside the system. A draft for an implementation lead doing heavy framing is overproduced for its reader. A draft for a governor's office that is mostly content with little framing underdelivers.

**The editorial-latitude gradient.** How pointed the work can be also shifts. Advocacy tolerates the most edge. Funders sit below that. Governor's office and agency leadership need the most measured, asset-based framing; being too critical of the system or the people in it backfires. Calibrate "appropriate edge" to where the named reader sits.

**Systems-change framing is not universal.** The architectural framing of what's broken and what must change applies most to governor's office, advocacy, and funder readers. With agency leadership and especially implementation leads, leading heavily with systems-change framing reads as overproduced or out of register. The work for those readers sits closer to the operational ground.

**Two of the five collapse most of the time.** Agency director and implementation lead are the same reader about 90% of the time. Treat them as variants unless the draft has a reason to separate them.

**Anxiety drives more correction than interest.** When framing backfires it usually does so by triggering an anxiety the analyst didn't account for: political exposure for a governor's office, implementation risk for an agency lead, ideological compromise for an advocacy partner. Check whether the draft handles the named reader's anxieties, not just their stated interests.

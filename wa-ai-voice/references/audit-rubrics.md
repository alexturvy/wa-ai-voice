# Audit Rubrics — Per Format

The five-question audit (INTERCHANGEABLE / ADDITIVE / SHAPED / VOICED / INSIGHT) applies everywhere. Each format below adds its own pass/fail checks on top. Run format checks AFTER the 5Q pass, before scoring.

## Client memo / brief / recommendation — the primary Watershed format

Read `watershed-ece-voice.md` first. These checks are on top of it.

- **The bottom line is the first thing on the page.** Not context, not method, not scope. If the reader stops after two sentences, they have the recommendation.
- **Claim then evidence, per section.** Any section that stacks findings and resolves at the end is inverted — flag the section, not the sentences.
- **Every section header earns its section.** Section count comes from the argument. Three sections because the argument has three parts, never because three is the shape.
- **Body carries the argument; detail goes to the appendix.** Methodology, full tables, rosters, the long version of a caveat. A body paragraph that would survive being moved to an appendix belongs in the appendix.
- **Named reader on the record.** Who this is for and what they should do after. If the draft can't say, that's the first finding.
- **The ask is explicit.** A recommendation memo that never states what it wants is a summary with a header.
- **INSIGHT is scored strictly here, and the unit is the claim plus what follows it.** A summary sentence is not defective in isolation — "Enrollment in state-funded pre-K has flattened in 14 states" is clean, specific, and passes every sentence-level check. The failure is that nothing after it says why, what caused it, or what to do. Read forward: flag when the paragraph never supplies the so-what, not when a sentence lacks it. An auditor working strictly sentence by sentence will miss every INSIGHT failure in the document.

**Overrides — these general rules are wrong for a memo and do not apply:**

1. *"The conclusion must do work the body didn't (a turn, a cost, a dare)"* (long-form, below) — a correctly BLUF'd memo has already delivered its conclusion. The closer restates the ask or names the next decision; that is not an ADDITIVE failure here.
2. *Density: one concrete particular per ~150 words* (long-form, below) — moving detail to the appendix thins the body on purpose. Judge density against the claims made, not against a word count: every claim in the body needs its support somewhere, and "in the appendix" is somewhere.
3. *Closer physics from `master-list.md` A2* — post-kicker coda, "the last line is the flattest and shortest beat," aphorism-closer bans. Those are social-post rules. A memo ends on the recommendation or the decision needed, and that line can be the longest on the page.

## Document-level pass — run once per deliverable, after the unit-by-unit audit

The five gates work on units and will clear a document whose failure is structural. Four questions, whole-document, two minutes:

1. **Where does this document admit it doesn't know something?** If there is no such place, the gaps were hidden rather than closed. A filled matrix with no "unknown" cell, a complete list of options with nothing marked uncertain, a taxonomy with no ragged edge — each was completed rather than researched. Real analysis is lumpy.

   *Worked example — the target:* PE-2 runs four hero numbers across the top of its opening section. Three are `-$2.6B`, `60%`, `43rd`. The fourth is `?`, labeled *"Status of kindergarten readiness for Tennessee children. Though a critical metric, Tennessee has no consistent way of evaluating or tracking kindergarten readiness."* The gap gets the same visual weight as the $2.6 billion and becomes an argument for the recommendation. Compare AE-3, which presented a complete architecture in the body and put five caveats in Appendix B. Promote the unknown; don't bury it.
2. **Is any signal of rigor unearned?** ID schemes nobody will ever say out loud (`per G-4…`), citations more precise than the source they came from, an appendix of caveats that withdraws what the body asserts, a label vocabulary the reader has to learn before reading. Individually defensible, collectively a document performing thoroughness.
3. **Where does it commit?** Options presented → one recommended. Open questions listed → ordered. Impact claimed → magnitude, direction, timeframe. If the answer is nowhere, volume is standing in for judgment, and the fix is a position, not an edit.
4. **What specifics did the polish delete?** Diff the deliverable against the notes or transcript it came from. Names, dates, numbers, and constraints that were in the source and aren't in the draft go back in. This is the single most recurrent failure in the observed corpus.

Also flag, without fixing: coined terms, category schemes, and acronym expansions. Those belong to the team.

## Board / leadership deck

Everything in the memo rubric, plus:

- One idea per slide, and the slide title states it as a claim. A title that names a topic ("Enrollment Trends") instead of making a claim ("Enrollment is flat because the ARPA slots expired") is the deck version of burying the lead.
- The title alone carries the argument if the deck is read without narration. Read the titles in sequence: they should be the memo.
- Body text supports the title claim or comes out. Slides do not hold appendix detail — the appendix does.
- Slide-title phrasing in prose is a tell (`tells.md` §1); on a slide it is the format. Do not flag it here.

## Original post / thread (social)

- Hook is a specific (moment, number, claim, image-in-words) — not question-bait ("what are you building?"), not a category label ("AI is moving fast").
- One thought per post. A thread is thoughts in sequence, not one thought stretched.
- No thread-boi scaffolding: no "1/ 🧵", no "let that sink in", no recap-post + CTA combo unless the bound author genuinely does that.
- The last line lands — it doesn't summarize. If the last line could open the piece, the ending is missing.
- Would the author post this with their name on it TODAY (not in 2022)? Dead formats are tells.
- Reply BATCHES are out of scope for this gate: they additionally need target selection and batch-level move variety.

## Long-form / article / newsletter

- A specific appears within the first two sentences. Throat-clearing openers ("In today's world…", scene-setting that sets no scene) fail.
- Section structure follows the argument, not a template. If the headings could organize any article on the topic, they're SHAPED failures.
- Density check: at least one concrete particular (number, name, scene, quote) per ~150 words. Long stretches of pure abstraction are where slop pools.
- Subheads in the author's register, not SEO Title Case (unless the author's platform demands it).
- The conclusion must do work the body didn't (a turn, a cost, a dare) — restating the sections is an automatic ADDITIVE fail.
- Quotes and citations are immune from tell-flagging, but check they're load-bearing, not decoration.

## Docs / README / technical writing

- No marketing adjectives on technical nouns: "powerful", "seamless", "blazing-fast", "robust" — show the benchmark or cut.
- Examples beat adjectives. Every claim about behavior gets a code block or command the reader can run.
- "Simply" / "just" / "easy" before instructions: condescension tells — delete; if it were simple the sentence wouldn't be needed.
- Steps must be executable as written (no "configure as appropriate").
- Voice rules relax here: docs legitimately standardize. VOICED is satisfied by the project's existing docs register, and uniform structure is fine — the SHAPED check inverts (consistency is the feature). Tells sections 1 and 4 still apply in full.

## Email / DM / message

- The ask (or the point) lands in the first two lines.
- No "I hope this email finds you well", no "I wanted to reach out", no "Just following up" as a content-free opener — open with the actual reason.
- Match the existing thread's register; a sudden formality jump reads as paste.
- Length floor: if it fits in two sentences, it's two sentences.
- Sign-offs in the author's habit, not "Best regards" by default.

## The score sheet (all formats)

Count per ~500 words (whole piece if shorter):

| Signal | Pts each | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tell cluster (3+ catalog tells within ~100 words) | 2 | singles are noise, log but don't score |
| INTERCHANGEABLE unit | 2 | the "20 unrelated pieces" test |
| VOICED failure | 2 | only when a voice is bound |
| INSIGHT failure | 2 | names a thing and stops; exempt in informational slots |
| SHAPED structure | 1 | template imposed on content |
| ADDITIVE failure | 1 | padding, restating, throat-clearing |

**Counting rules (all five, no exceptions):**

0. **Read forward before scoring anything.** A unit followed by its anchor is scored as the pair, not alone. This governs every question below and it is the single most common way an audit manufactures false findings.
   - *INTERCHANGEABLE*: a general claim immediately cashed out with a named specific passes. The house pattern is claim → `Current State:` receipt (`exemplars.md` PE-2). Scored in isolation, every benefit sentence in the strongest deliverable in the corpus fails.
   - *INSIGHT*: the unit is the claim plus what follows it. "Enrollment has flattened in 14 states" is a clean sentence; the failure is that nothing after it says why.
   - *The apparatus tell* is invisible at unit level entirely — it lives in the document-level pass above.

   If you are scoring sentence by sentence with no forward look, you are finding the wrong things and missing the real ones.

1. One unit scores its single worst failure — no double-counting a sentence that is both interchangeable and unvoiced.
2. **A span eats its units**: a scored tell cluster absorbs the 5Q failures of the sentences inside it. Score the cluster's 2 points; the sentences inside add nothing. Without this rule the same bad paragraph scores 6+ and the number stops meaning anything.
3. **Piece-level VOICED**: if more than half the units fail VOICED, stop counting them — score VOICED once (+2) for the piece and route to a full rewrite in the bound voice instead of span fixes. Patching a wholly unvoiced piece span-by-span produces Frankenstein text.
4. **Piece-level INSIGHT**: same shape. If more than half the units fail INSIGHT, score it once (+2) and fix the argument, not the spans. A piece that is locally specific everywhere and says nothing overall fails at the level of what it is for — sentence surgery will not reach it. Report it that way: "this is a summary; it needs a point of view before it needs edits."

Sum, cap at 10. Ship at ≤2. Maximum two fix passes, then ship or escalate to the user — a third pass over-sands.

**A capped score is a route, not a rank.** Once a piece hits 10 the number stops discriminating — two slides can both cap while one needs three fixes and the other needs to be thrown out. When the count reaches the cap, don't report "10." Report the route: *"caps out — this needs an argument before it needs edits."* Then name the two or three failures that would have to be fixed first. Coaching needs the direction, not the ceiling.

**Scoring decks:** the unit is the slide, and the deck is the piece. Score slide by slide, then report the deck-level pattern. Per-500-word normalization is meaningless on a 40-word slide — a slide of eight empty bullets is a failed slide, not a low-density one.

Report honestly: `slop score 7 → 1 (2 clusters, 1 interchangeable fixed; 1 single tell left as voice-consistent)`. Never report a score you didn't count.

## Audit-mode prerequisite: establish authorship first

Before flagging anything in AUDIT mode, determine whose text this is. If it's a real human's writing (the user's, a client's), **bind that author's voice first** — their observed rates override the default caps, and their signature shapes are protected (see tells.md). Running default caps on known-human text manufactures false positives: a real author's triplet joke is voice, not a rule-of-three tell. Default caps exist for unbound machine output only.

## Audit-mode output format

When auditing existing text, return:
1. Score before, with the counted list (quote each flagged span, name the failure).
2. The rewrite (or per-span fixes for long pieces).
3. Score after.
4. Anything left unfixed and why (no voice precedent, quoted material, author habit).

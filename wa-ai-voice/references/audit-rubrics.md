# Audit Rubrics — Per Format

The five-question audit (INTERCHANGEABLE / ADDITIVE / SHAPED / VOICED / INSIGHT) applies everywhere, and the fourteen named failures in `watershed-ece-voice.md` apply everywhere. Each deliverable type below adds its own checks and sets its own bar. Run format checks AFTER the 5Q pass, before reporting.

**The bar varies by type, and that is the point.** Evaluating a running agenda deck against culminating-report standards produces feedback that misses what the type is for. Name the type before you start.

## Culminating report

The polished deliverable — published by the client or by Watershed. The closing artifact of an engagement. **Highest bar of any type**, for language, incisiveness, and sentence-level care.

- Bottom line first. If the reader stops after two sentences, they have the recommendation.
- Claim then evidence, per section. Any section that stacks findings and resolves at the end is inverted; flag the section, not the sentences.
- Every section header earns its section. Section count comes from the argument, never from a shape.
- Body carries the argument; methodology, full tables, and rosters go to an appendix.
- Named reader on the record, and the editorial register calibrated to that reader (`audience-archetypes.md`). A report for advocacy partners can carry edge that would backfire with an agency lead. Pitched at the wrong level of edge is a finding even when the prose is clean.
- The ask is explicit. A recommendation report that never states what it wants is a summary with a header.
- **Is it driving its points cleanly, or burying them?** Sentence-level care is in scope here in a way it is not for the working types.
- Could this be published externally with no further editing? If not, say what stands in the way.
- Insight is judged strictly, reading forward: the unit is the claim plus what follows it.

**Overrides — these general rules are wrong here and do not apply:**

1. *"The conclusion must do work the body didn't"* — a correctly BLUF'd report has already delivered its conclusion. The closer restates the ask or names the next decision. Not an ADDITIVE failure.
2. *Density: one concrete particular per ~150 words* — appendix discipline thins the body on purpose. Judge density against the claims made: every claim needs its support somewhere, and "in the appendix" is somewhere.
3. *Closer physics from `master-list.md` A2* (post-kicker coda, shortest-last-line, aphorism-closer bans). Those are social-post rules. A report ends on the recommendation or the decision needed, and that line can be the longest on the page.

Visual and design polish is out of scope; that is handled separately.

## Running agenda deck

The working-session deck for a regular client call — often the first time a concept is put in front of the client. Built for reaction, not polish. **Lower bar than a culminating report, but a real one**: these get corrected, even when everyone wishes they didn't.

- Each slide is oriented toward a decision, a reaction, or a building block in the engagement. Status reporting is the failure mode.
- **The slide signals what the analyst wants the client to weigh in on.** These are collaborative by design; the most common miss is not being clear what specifically the client should react to. For every slide, the analyst should know what they want out of putting it in front of the client.
- Recommendations are grounded in what we know about this state, not generic.
- Language is clean enough for the audience even at the lower bar.
- One idea per slide, and the title states it as a claim. "Enrollment Trends" is the deck version of burying the lead; "Enrollment is flat because the ARPA slots expired" is not. Read the titles in sequence: they should be the argument.
- Slide-title phrasing in prose is a tell (`tells.md` §1); on a slide it is the format. Do not flag it here.

## Stakeholder facilitation deck

A deck for facilitating a working group of external stakeholders, not an internal client meeting. **High bar**, because it has to function live in a room with people who lack the client team's context. It is doing change management, not just communicating findings.

- Is the room actually identified — who is in it, what they need, what they are skeptical of? Their resistance accounted for?
- Does the deck bring people along, or does it present at them? Change management is the work.
- Is the framing pitched to move people who may not be predisposed to agree, rather than to inform people who already do?
- Does it use the time well — interactive structures, not two hours of slides?
- Is "what's next" clear? Does this string into another engagement, lead to a report, or end here? People need to know what becomes of their participation.

## Planning tool

Project plans, scopes and sequences, Gantt charts — often something the client will use directly. **The bar is functional, not editorial**, and functionality is not trivial. A planning tool that doesn't work is worse than no tool.

- Could the client use this without you in the room?
- Is the sequence of activities logical, or modeled on a template that doesn't fit this engagement?
- Are the timelines realistic given state engagement pace? Time is the dominant constraint; unrealistic timelines undermine the whole artifact.
- Does the plan actually do what the engagement needs?
- Is each element doing work, or is there filler?

**Watch for pattern-matching.** Newer analysts often model sequencing on an older example from a different state. Structurally right, wrong for this engagement.

## Implementation support tool

Tactical artifacts built while doing the work rather than presenting it: SOPs, data collection tools, surveys, decision sandboxes, coordinated enrollment or request systems. Things the client copies and uses with their field. **Bar: functional and strategically aligned.**

- Does the tool work mechanically — can the client use it as built?
- **Are the constraints clearly modeled, with real tradeoffs visible?** Many of these work by modeling constraints and handing the client a genuine decision. Check that it gives a decision rather than pre-deciding.
- Is it aligned to the engagement's broader strategy, or has it drifted into pure execution? A tool that works mechanically but pulls against the engagement's direction is a problem.
- Same clarity and soundness checks as a planning tool.

## Client email and internal note

- The ask, or the point, lands in the first two lines.
- No "I hope this email finds you well", no "I wanted to reach out", no "Just following up" as a content-free opener. Open with the actual reason.
- Match the thread's register; a sudden formality jump reads as paste.
- Length floor: if it fits in two sentences, it is two sentences.
- **The Part 3 protected rates do not apply here.** They were observed in published reports. Do not defend 20–35 word formal sentences in a three-line email.

## Cross-cutting

- **Collaboration register varies.** Running agenda decks are explicitly collaborative. Culminating reports are presented, not co-written. Facilitation decks are collaborative in a live-room way. Planning and implementation tools are handed over. Check the draft is structured for the right kind of interaction.
- **Strategic alignment holds at every level.** Even an SOP or a survey should advance the engagement's direction rather than execute a discrete task.
- **Pattern-matching against old examples** shows up most in planning and implementation tools but can appear anywhere: sequencing from another state's timeline, constraints copied from an unrelated tool, language lifted from a previous report without re-grounding.

## Document-level pass — run once per deliverable, after the unit-by-unit audit

The five gates work on units and will clear a document whose failure is structural. Four questions, whole-document, two minutes:

1. **Where does this document admit it doesn't know something?** If there is no such place, the gaps were hidden rather than closed. A filled matrix with no "unknown" cell, a complete list of options with nothing marked uncertain, a taxonomy with no ragged edge — each was completed rather than researched. Real analysis is lumpy.

   *Worked example — the target:* PE-2 runs four hero numbers across the top of its opening section. Three are `-$2.6B`, `60%`, `43rd`. The fourth is `?`, labeled *"Status of kindergarten readiness for Tennessee children. Though a critical metric, Tennessee has no consistent way of evaluating or tracking kindergarten readiness."* The gap gets the same visual weight as the $2.6 billion and becomes an argument for the recommendation. Compare AE-3, which presented a complete architecture in the body and put its caveats in a validation appendix. Promote the unknown; don't bury it.
2. **Is any signal of rigor unearned?** ID schemes nobody will ever say out loud (`per G-4…`), citations more precise than the source they came from, an appendix of caveats that withdraws what the body asserts, a label vocabulary the reader has to learn before reading. Individually defensible, collectively a document performing thoroughness.
3. **Where does it commit?** Options presented → one recommended. Open questions listed → ordered. Impact claimed → magnitude, direction, timeframe. If the answer is nowhere, volume is standing in for judgment, and the fix is a position, not an edit.
4. **What specifics did the polish delete?** Diff the deliverable against the notes or transcript it came from. Names, dates, numbers, and constraints that were in the source and aren't in the draft go back in. This is the single most recurrent failure in the observed corpus.

Also flag, without fixing: coined terms, category schemes, and acronym expansions. Those belong to the team.

## Long-form article or newsletter

- A specific appears within the first two sentences. Throat-clearing openers fail.
- Section structure follows the argument. Headings that could organize any article on the topic are SHAPED failures.
- Subheads in the author's register, not SEO Title Case.
- The conclusion does work the body didn't. Restating the sections is an ADDITIVE fail.
- Quotes and citations are immune from tell-flagging, but check they're load-bearing.

## Docs and technical writing

- No marketing adjectives on technical nouns: "powerful", "seamless", "robust". Show the benchmark or cut.
- Examples beat adjectives. Every behavior claim gets a command the reader can run.
- "Simply" / "just" / "easy" before instructions: delete. If it were simple the sentence wouldn't be needed.
- Steps executable as written, no "configure as appropriate".
- Voice rules relax: docs legitimately standardize. VOICED is satisfied by the project's existing register and the SHAPED check inverts, since consistency is the feature. Tells sections 1 and 4 still apply.

## The findings list (all formats)

Report failures by category, quoting each flagged span. **No weights, no sum, no 0–10 scale** — see `../SKILL.md` Layer 3 for why the arithmetic was removed.

| Category | What it is |
|---|---|
| Always-strip | em-dash, or "not X but Y". **Exempt from the cluster rule: every instance counts on its own.** No observed rate overrides these |
| Tell cluster | 3+ catalog tells within ~100 words; singles are noise, log but don't count |
| INTERCHANGEABLE | the "20 unrelated pieces" test |
| VOICED | only when a voice is bound |
| INSIGHT | names a thing and stops; exempt in informational slots |
| SHAPED | template imposed on content |
| ADDITIVE | padding, restating, throat-clearing |

**Counting rules (all five, no exceptions):**

0. **Read forward before counting anything.** A unit followed by its anchor counts as the pair, not alone. This governs every category and it is the single most common way an audit manufactures false findings.
   - *INTERCHANGEABLE*: a general claim immediately cashed out with a named specific passes. The house pattern is claim → `Current State:` receipt (`exemplars.md` PE-2). Counted in isolation, every benefit sentence in the strongest deliverable in the corpus fails.
   - *INSIGHT*: the unit is the claim plus what follows it. "Enrollment has flattened in 14 states" is a clean sentence; the failure is that nothing after it says why.
   - *The apparatus tell* is invisible at unit level entirely — it lives in the document-level pass above.

   If you are working sentence by sentence with no forward look, you are finding the wrong things and missing the real ones.

1. One unit counts once, under its worst failure — no double-listing a sentence that is both interchangeable and unvoiced.
2. **A span eats its units**: a tell cluster absorbs the failures of the sentences inside it. List the cluster; the sentences inside add nothing. Without this rule one bad paragraph generates six findings and the list stops meaning anything.
3. **Piece-level VOICED**: when the piece fails as a whole rather than in spots, stop listing units. Report one piece-level finding, name which load-bearing voice features it misses, and quote two spans that show it. Route to a full rewrite in the bound voice. Do not gate this on a fraction of units — a ratio over a judgment that does not replicate is the numeric score returning through the back door (`master-list.md` PART D: require every verdict to state what decided it). Patching a wholly unvoiced piece span-by-span produces Frankenstein text.
4. **Piece-level INSIGHT**: same shape, judged the same way — state what decided it, don't count to a threshold. A piece that is locally specific everywhere and says nothing overall fails at the level of what it is for, and sentence surgery will not reach it. Report it that way: *"this is a summary; it needs a point of view before it needs edits."*

**The verdict.** Not a number — one of three: **Ship** (no piece-level failure fired and every span still flagged has a stated reason for staying) · **Fix these** (named spans, each with its failure and an in-voice replacement) · **Rewrite the argument** (a piece-level failure fired). Maximum two fix passes either way, then ship or escalate — a third pass over-sands.

Report honestly: `4 findings — 2 tell clusters, 1 interchangeable, 1 additive; 1 single tell left as voice-consistent. Verdict: fix these.` Never report a finding you didn't locate in the text.

**Decks:** the unit is the slide, the piece is the deck. Report slide by slide, then the deck-level pattern.

**Pieces over ~3,000 words:** audit section by section rather than attempting the whole document in one pass. Carry a running per-category tally, name every section you covered, and run the document-level pass on the whole piece only after the section sweep finishes. Two of the three calibration exemplars are 40–50 page reports; without decomposition the pass silently ends wherever it ran out of room, and a partial audit reads exactly like a complete one.

## Audit-mode prerequisite: establish authorship first

Before flagging anything in AUDIT mode, determine whose text this is. If it's a real human's writing (the user's, a client's), **bind that author's voice first** — their observed rates override the default caps, and their signature shapes are protected (see tells.md). Running default caps on known-human text manufactures false positives: a real author's triplet joke is voice, not a rule-of-three tell. Default caps exist for unbound machine output only.

## Audit-mode output format

When auditing existing text, return:
1. The findings before, quoting each flagged span and naming the failure.
2. The verdict: **ship**, **fix these**, or **rewrite the argument**.
3. The rewrite (or per-span fixes for long pieces).
4. The counted findings after, by category.
5. Anything left unfixed and why (no voice precedent, quoted material, author habit).

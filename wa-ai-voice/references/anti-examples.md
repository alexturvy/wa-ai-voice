# Anti-examples — observed failures in real Watershed work product

Anti-anchors, not a rulebook. Each entry quotes the span, names the failure, and says what the fix would have needed. Rules get derived once there are enough entries to see a pattern across pieces — a single piece is below the n threshold that PART B requires before anything becomes a rule.

**De-identified for internal circulation.** No authors, clients, vendors, states, agencies, or statutory citations are named, and no verbatim internal review commentary or unpublished internal notes are reproduced — those are paraphrased. Quoted spans are limited to text that is generic by construction, which is the property being demonstrated. What matters is the failure mode, never who produced it.

**The spine of this file, in one sentence: all three pieces avoid the moment of judgment, and each uses a different disguise.** AE-1 avoids it with warmth, AE-2 with a complete menu of options, AE-3 with a complete apparatus of structure. None of them says "here is what we think you should do, and here is the risk if we're wrong." Three documents that look nothing alike, one failure.

## AE-1 — State QRIS pilot deck, 6 slides, 2026

Client-facing deck on a CLASS-based redesign of a state quality rating system: what changes in the pilot year, who participates, vendor resources available, anticipated impact. Slide 1 is internal planning notes; slides 2–6 are the client-facing product.

### The finding that matters most: formalizing removed the content

The instinctive read is "the analyst had no facts, so they wrote filler." That's wrong, and the truth is more useful.

**Slide 1 — the unpolished internal notes — is the densest writing in the deck.** Lowercase, fragmentary, unedited, and full of real material: a named conference to schedule around, a specific multi-month window, the actual times of day sessions could run, second-language training needs for one provider type, accreditation concerns specific to another, and a request to pull comparable approaches from other states.

**Slides 4–6 — the polished client slides — contain none of it.** The specifics didn't fail to exist. They existed on slide 1 and did not survive the transition to client-facing prose. Direct pairs:

| In the internal notes (paraphrased) | What it became |
|---|---|
| a specific scheduling window, the times of day sessions could realistically run, and a named conference to avoid | a slide titled **"What's Next"** containing no date |
| which training sessions were needed in a second language, and which topics mattered to one specific provider type | a generic list of vendor offerings; no language availability, no provider-type distinction |
| what providers needed to understand before an observation versus after it | a bullet reading "get real data" |
| a specific accreditation concern raised by one participant category | that participant type listed on a roster; the concern gone |

The diagnosis an analyst can act on: **the specifics were already in your notes. Polishing is what deleted them.** Formalizing pulled every sentence up an altitude, and at that altitude nothing is specific to this state, this pilot, or this year.

### Per-slide

**Slide 2 — plan for the pilot year. Score: 3.** The strongest slide, and worth studying for that. Real content: pilot providers receive a CLASS observation in place of the legacy CQI visit; non-participants choose between freezing their rating and a pared-down annual review; the freeze runs until new ratings publish, roughly three years out.

- *SHAPED (1)* — title states a topic ("2026-2027 Plan for [the program]"), not the claim.
- *ADDITIVE (1, ×2)* — both options are stated, then restated in expanded form below. Say each once, fully. The restatements do carry new facts ("annually", "a limited number of standards"), which is why this reads as thorough rather than repetitive — it's still two passes at one idea.
- Left alone: "likely 3 years from now" hedges the most decision-relevant number in the deck, but the hedge may be honest. Flag for the author, don't fix.

**Slide 3 — implementation decisions. Score: capped.**

- *Title/content mismatch* — "Implementation Decision Points" contains no decision point. Every line states a decision already made or a plan already forming. If a slide promises decisions, it names the choice and who makes it.
- *Orphaned enumeration* — "[Agency] staff will be split across three distinct functions" is the first bullet; the three functions are the last three bullets, separated by unrelated content about bonuses and scholarships. The stem and its items are on opposite ends of the slide.
- *Label-only slot* — "Scholarship Funding" appears as a bare heading with nothing under it. An unfinished section shipped as finished. This is the bracket-placeholder failure in a different costume.
- *Buried lead in a single sentence* — 60 words beginning "[The agency] is developing a transition and support plan that ensures..." The load-bearing facts (programs will drop ratings; there's a 2–3 year hold harmless) arrive mid-sentence, behind a subject that is the plan rather than the consequence. Closes on a rule-of-three verb chain: "strengthen adult–child interactions, improve CLASS scores, and regain higher rating levels over time."
- *Undefined hedge* — "may allow quality bonuses to continue for a defined period." The period is not defined. A defined period that isn't defined is worse than no claim.

**Slide 4 — participants and priorities. Score: capped.**

- The four priorities — "Actionable feedback for educators," "Alignment across system actors," "A clear picture of quality over time," "Accountability for public investment" — are four noun phrases that could head the priorities list of any state QRIS redesign in the country. *INTERCHANGEABLE* on all four. Two filler-lexicon hits ("actionable," "alignment" in the non-governance sense).
- *INSIGHT* — names priorities without saying why these four, what they cost, or what they trade against each other. Four priorities with no ranking is zero priorities.
- The participant roster (lead entity, regional partners, feedback body, approving board) is a directory. Directories are appendix material unless the point is who's missing or who's overloaded.

**Slide 5 — vendor resources. Score: capped.**

- *Vendor voice leaking in* — "[Vendor] offers a mature, dual-track CLASS toolkit today." That's the vendor's sales sheet restated. "Mature" and "toolkit" are the vendor's adjectives. Watershed's job on a vendor slide is the assessment: which of these the state should use, in what sequence, and what's missing.
- *Feature list* — five offerings enumerated, none evaluated. "New leadership team" is listed as an available resource, which it isn't.
- *Two titles, one slide* — "What's Next" over "[Vendor] Resources Available Overview." Neither states a claim, and the first is contradicted by the absence of any date.

**Slide 6 — anticipated impact. Score: capped, and the worst in the deck.**

Eight bullets, two columns of four, ~40 words total:

> For Providers: Join the pilot, get real data · Less paperwork, more coaching · A protected transition · Support matched to need
> For Families: A statewide view of quality · Ratings families can trust · Transparent, public reporting · A stronger start for every child

- *INTERCHANGEABLE on all eight.* Not one names this state, this pilot, this year, or this vendor. Every line would sit unchanged in any early childhood quality initiative in any state.
- *Impact claim with no impact.* The slide is titled "Anticipated Impact" and contains no magnitude, no direction, and no timeframe. An impact claim needs how much, which way, by when — otherwise it is a values statement with a chart's title.
- *Manufactured cadence.* Eight bullets sharing one grammatical shape and near-identical length, arranged 4×4. The shape was chosen before the content; the content was then cut to fit it. The symmetry is the tell.
- *Brochure register.* "Join the pilot, get real data" is ad copy. "A stronger start for every child" is a mission statement — the fortune-cookie failure: clean, warm, quotable, and true of every ECE program that has ever existed.
- Piece-level *INSIGHT* fires. This slide needs an argument before it needs edits.

### What the terms-of-art carve-out got right

CLASS, CQI, CCRC, LEA, FCC, hold harmless, and the state program name all appear repeatedly and none should be flagged — none are on the explicit terms-of-art list in `watershed-ece-voice.md` §4, and the fallback test ("would the reader lose information in plain language?") carried all of them. Meanwhile "actionable" and "alignment" fired correctly as filler in the same document. The split works. Promote these terms to the explicit list.

## AE-2 — Deep-dive synthesis memo, ~1,100 words, 2026

Written after a working session on prequalification requirements for a state universal pre-K system. Structure: a five-bullet synthesis, six numbered requirement areas each with labeled sub-points, a deferred-items list, and an action log.

**This one is dangerous because it is competent.** Unlike AE-1 it is dense, well-organized, factually careful, and specific about the domain. It fails at the one thing that makes it worth paying for: after 1,100 words, the reader does not know what the firm thinks should happen.

- **Nothing is ranked.** Roughly ten items are labeled "Open." Not one is marked more urgent, more consequential, or more likely to block the others. A list of ten open questions with no ordering hands the prioritization back to the client, which is the work they hired out.
- **The posture is stenographic.** "Participants did not identify a problem with initial verification" appears twice. The document reports what a room said rather than what the firm concludes from it. That is a faithful transcript, and a transcript is not advice.
- **Content-free directives.** "Strengthen — Narrow or prioritize expectations where possible." Under a heading that promises action, a sentence that specifies no action, no target, and no criterion for "where possible." Several labels in this document govern text that says nothing.
- **Invented taxonomy.** Nine distinct label types across six sections: *Working direction · Current-state finding · Strengthen · Align · Clarify and align · Open · Design direction · Near-term alignment option · Options requiring further development.* The reader has to learn a vocabulary to read a memo. Two of those labels are indistinguishable in practice.
- **Template applied past its content.** Sections 5 and 6 have only "Working direction" — one line each — while the template's other slots stand empty. That's the shape of the document declaring itself before the content arrived.
- **Abstraction stack.** "The needed changes are primarily in evidence, verification, communication, and support." Four nouns, no referents. What is a change "in support"?
- **"Meaningful" four times** — "meaningful quality improvement," "meaningful classroom-quality information," "meaningful assurance," "meaningful evidence." It is doing the work a number should do.
- **Hedge density.** "may be more useful," "may be a nearer-term option," "wherever it can satisfy," "where possible," "generally works." The synthesis of a completed working session should have fewer hedges than the agenda that preceded it, not more.
- **No numbers, no names, no dates.** One duration appears in the entire document ("a three-year cycle"). The action log assigns five actions and gives not one of them a due date.
- Opening line — "The group focused on making the system more coherent—not lowering expectations for quality" — is defensive framing, plus the negative-parallelism tell. It leads with a reassurance about what the work is *not*. The actual bottom line is buried in bullet four: nobody knows what is happening in classrooms, and paper submissions won't tell them.

## AE-3 — Interagency regulatory clarification draft, ~5,500 words, 2026

A working draft proposing a joint compliance architecture across two state agencies: purpose and decision rules, a twelve-row regulatory crosswalk, an evidence-acceptance framework, a five-part amendment package, an implementation sequence, eight questions for resolution, two appendices. It carries thirteen reviewer comments, which makes it the only piece in this file with real calibration data attached.

**Credit first, because it earns it.** Its opening "Recommended direction" section is a genuine bottom-line-up-front — the whole architecture stated in two sentences before any supporting material, followed by five concrete moves. It is the clearest example of the structure the house voice asks for in any of these three pieces. The failure is downstream of it.

### The apparatus tell

This is the document's signature failure, and it is new to the catalog: **the apparatus of rigor substituting for rigor.** Every visible signal of thoroughness is present and none of them was earned.

- **Five ID namespaces.** Five separate prefixed numbering schemes — one for crosswalk rows, one for practice changes, one for guidance products, one for rule amendments, one for statutory concepts — plus numbered open questions. Identifiers imply a managed system where people say "let's revisit G-4." No one has ever said that. The IDs were assigned at drafting, not adopted in use.
- **No empty cells.** A twelve-row × six-column crosswalk, every cell filled, every cell roughly the same length. Real research is lumpy: some intersections are well understood, some are unknown, some don't apply. A matrix with no gaps was completed, not investigated. The honest version has "unknown" in it.
- **Round completeness.** Twelve crosswalk rows, six practice changes, six guidance products, seven rule amendments, four statutory amendments, four phases, six measures, eight questions. Every list closes cleanly.
- **Precision beyond the evidence.** Section-level citations throughout, down to subsection ranges and page numbers in a program guide. A validation appendix then discloses that the sources used were third-party reproductions requiring comparison against official filings. The pinpoint citations are more precise than the material they came from. This is the deepest tell in the document: decimal-point precision is read as verification, and here it is decoration.
- **Hedge armor.** Counsel should confirm the legal vehicle; this is not drafting language; it is a working tool and not legal advice; validate everything against the official filing. Plus five more caveats in a validation appendix. The document asserts a full architecture and disclaims responsibility for every part of it. Confidence and liability move in opposite directions, which is how you can tell the confidence isn't real.

**The apparatus is invisible to the audit, which is why it needs a document-level check.** Tested: one crosswalk row passes all five gates — it names a specific system of record, commits to using it, reads in-voice, is not interchangeable, and directs the reader. It is also the row the reviewer flagged as factually wrong. A row-by-row audit clears this document. Only the whole-document view catches it.

### What the reviewer caught that the skill cannot

Thirteen comments, paraphrased and grouped by what catching them would require:

**Domain knowledge — invisible to any audit (4 comments):**
- A proposed workflow duplicated one that already exists. The draft didn't know the existing process.
- A rule was described using language the rule no longer uses. Stale fact, confidently stated.
- A recommendation had no motivating problem behind it.
- **The draft took a firm position nobody had agreed to.** The reviewer's note was a question: are we actually pushing for this? An AI draft resolves an open strategic question by writing past it, and the resolution reads as settled because it sits in the same register as everything else.

**Terminology and category governance — cheap to check (4 comments):**
- Check the categories for accuracy, align naming conventions, get the categories approved; expand acronyms on first use; two rows conflate things that should stay separate. The drafter settled naming and categories that belong to the team.

**Structure — catchable (3 comments):**
- **False hierarchy.** The reviewer asked why five items were nested as sub-points under one recommendation when they read as five separate recommendations. Nesting hid four of them.
- **Wrong artifact.** A four-condition decision framework belonged on a slide, not in a written deliverable.
- One unqualified "keep" — the only unreserved approval in the document.

A note for anyone reproducing this analysis: some cells in the exported file contained reviewer edits flattened into the body text, plus paste junk at the top. Those are export artifacts, not drafting failures, and are not counted above.

## Derived rules — adopted

Three pieces, and the recurrences are clear enough to promote. Everything that appeared in only one piece stays unadopted.

**1. FEED — formalizing deletes the specifics.** AE-1: internal notes name a conference, a date window, a language need; the polished slides name none of them. AE-2: a synthesis of a real working session containing no numbers, no names, and one duration. The specifics existed in both cases and did not survive the pass that made the document presentable. *Check:* before shipping, diff the deliverable against the notes it came from. Anything specific that dropped out, put back.

**2. INSIGHT — comprehensiveness is how a draft avoids committing.** AE-2 lists ten open questions and ranks none. AE-3 builds five complete taxonomies and disclaims all of them. AE-1 offers eight benefits and quantifies none. In each case the volume of the deliverable moves inversely to its willingness to choose. *Check:* if the document presents options, one is recommended. If it lists open questions, they're ordered. If it names an impact, it has a magnitude, a direction, and a timeframe.

**3. The apparatus tell — signals of rigor in place of rigor.** Filled matrices with no gaps, ID schemes nobody uses, pinpoint citations from approximate sources, label taxonomies the reader must learn, caveat appendices that disclaim the argument. Each is individually defensible; together they are a document performing thoroughness. *Check, at document level, not per unit:* Does any cell say "unknown"? Would anyone ever say this ID out loud? Is the citation more precise than the source it came from? Does the caveat section withdraw what the body asserts? Strongest single question — **where does this document admit it doesn't know something?** A deliverable with no such place has hidden its gaps rather than closed them.

**4. Terminology and categories belong to the team, not the drafter.** Four of thirteen reviewer comments on AE-3. Naming conventions, acronym expansions, and category boundaries settled silently in a draft become expensive to unwind once the client has read them. *Check:* flag every coined term, category scheme, and acronym on first use for confirmation.

### Held — appeared once, not adopted

Label-only sections shipped as finished (AE-1) · orphaned enumeration (AE-1) · source voice leaking in when summarizing a vendor or a statute (AE-1, weakly AE-3) · false hierarchy (AE-3) · content-free directives under action labels (AE-2) · invented label taxonomy (AE-2; overlaps the apparatus tell and may fold into it).
